# Architecture and Water Comparative Plate 0.1

**Control family:** `PD-004`

**Specialist gates:** `SR-006` — early Tamil institutions; `SR-010` — archaeology and material reconstruction

**Status:** `[INTERPRETATION]` — **NOT AN EXACT ANCIENT CITY PLAN**

## Purpose

This plate distinguishes Puhar, Vanji and Kanchi through function, water access, maintenance and institutional pressure. It prevents one monumental architectural language from being copied across all three cities.

## Comparative control

| City | Production anchor | Water story | Architectural emphasis | Camera rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `A1 — Puhar` | River–sea port and distributed harbour system | Abundant movement, uneven potable access, wharf and service demand | Mixed brick, timber, plaster, tile and thatch; cargo and festival routes | Show linked functions and working edges; no monumental stone harbour |
| `A2 — Vanji` | Provisional inland river/trade corridor | River-linked storage, caravan supply and compact institutions | Inland administrative, teaching and storage spaces; materially distinct from coast | Emphasise thresholds, debate and route exchange; no copied Puhar waterfront |
| `A3 — Kanchi` | Early historic inland political and religious centre | Failing wells, storage and access become famine evidence | Relief, teaching, royal decision and public feeding infrastructure | Let deprivation alter maintenance and movement; no later Pallava/Chola skyline |

## Shared material grammar

- fired and unfired brick only where function and comparison justify it;
- timber posts, beams and rafters remain visible parts of construction and repair;
- plaster is renewed, patched and weathered rather than permanently pristine;
- tile and thatch vary by access, function and maintenance;
- stone is functional or supported, not the automatic dominant material;
- service routes, waste, shade, storage and water carrying remain visible.

## Water states

| Code | Screen condition | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `W-A` | moving river, harbour or channel water | transport and labour; not automatically potable |
| `W-B` | household or neighbourhood collection | access shaped by carrying work, queue and status |
| `W-C` | stored water in jars, basins or tanks | finite capacity, cleaning and custody |
| `W-D` | pond or sacred water feature | function remains context-dependent |
| `W-E` | stressed or failed access | empty vessels, longer routes, rationing and conflict |
| `W-F` | relief water system | distribution, translation, sanitation and public record |

No ring well may be automatically labelled a drinking well, sewer, soakage pit, toilet or industrial installation. Its use remains unresolved without context and specialist review.

## City-specific prohibitions

### Puhar

- no single enclosed imperial harbour;
- no clean waterfront without repair, loading, sediment or service work;
- no assumption that every reported offshore remain belongs to one literary city phase.

### Vanji

- no assertion that the provisional Karur anchor supplies a complete exact plan;
- no coastal harbour spectacle imported inland;
- no single “Chera style” invented from later royal imagery.

### Kanchi

- no later Pallava temple or imperial Chola tower as the default skyline;
- no intact prosperous city merely decorated with a few hungry extras;
- no aqueduct, sewer grid or monumental palace without evidence.

## Continuity rules

1. Architecture changes through use, repair, crowd pressure, season and institutional choice.
2. Water props travel through identifiable clean, used and withheld states.
3. Famine appears first in access, maintenance, storage and labour—not only bodies.
4. Royal power is shown through thresholds, records, guards, ration control and access.
5. Feeding and prison reform require water, kitchens, cleaning, sleep and public accountability.
6. The same ring-well form cannot silently receive different convenient functions across scenes.
